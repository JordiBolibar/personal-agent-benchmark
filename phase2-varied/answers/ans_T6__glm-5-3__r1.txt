## 1. Volume response time τ_V = H/(−b_t)

Start from the lumped ice-equivalent budget of the whole glacier:

- dV/dt = B(t) = ∫b dA, with V = H·A (mean thickness times area). Steady state: B = 0.
- After a step change to a warmer climate, assume the area-averaged specific balance of the ice still to be lost is ⟨b⟩ ≈ b_t (the terminus value): the ablation zone dominates the integrated deficit (Jóhanneson, Raymond & Waddington 1989).
- Assume self-similar retreat, so the remaining deficit is proportional to the remaining excess thickness: ⟨b(t)⟩ = b_t · (δH/H), where δH = H − H_steady,new.
- Then dδH/dt = b_t δH/H, i.e. exponential relaxation with

**τ_V = H/(−b_t) = 150 m / 5 m a⁻¹ = 30 a.**

Intuition: the time to melt one full ice column at the terminus melt rate.

Assumptions (all of them):
1. All balances in ice equivalent; constant density; volume changes only via surface balance (no calving, no basal melt anomalies).
2. Step change to a new, constant climate; the balance–elevation profile only shifts; no elevation feedback (deferred to §3).
3. ⟨b⟩ ≈ b_t, and b_t stays −5 m a⁻¹ as the terminus moves.
4. Shape-preserving adjustment: area, shape and H ≈ 150 m stay fixed over one e-fold.
5. Dynamics are not the bottleneck (checked in §2).
6. Linear perturbation regime.
7. No sliding change, constant rheology, fixed bed.

Note what is absent: τ_V contains no L, β, n or flow factor. It holds only as long as assumptions 3–5 hold, and it is a *volume* scale: the full geometry (length) adjustment is much slower (§2).

## 2. Kinematic wave speed; advective and diffusive time scales

SIA flux per unit width (1-D, slope s = −∂S/∂x):

q = (2A/(n+2)) (ρg)ⁿ H^(n+2) sⁿ

Continuity, with q = q(H, s):

∂H/∂t + c ∂H/∂x − D ∂²H/∂x² = b,  c = ∂q/∂H = (n+2) q/H,  D = ∂q/∂s = n q/s

Why c > mean ice speed: the wave travels at the *marginal* flux gain per added metre of thickness. Since q ∝ H^(n+2), a locally thickened patch carries proportionally more flux (its velocity also rises, u ∝ H^(n+1)); the wave rides this excess discharge, giving c = (n+2)ū. Ice parcels move slower and pass through the wave.

Numbers (deformation-only, no basal sliding): u_s/ū = (n+2)/(n+1) = 5/4, so ū = 32 m a⁻¹, q = ū·H = 4.8×10³ m² a⁻¹, c = 5 × 32 = 160 m a⁻¹ ≈ 4 u_s. (With plug sliding c → ū ≈ u_s; 160 m a⁻¹ is the deformation-only upper end.)

**τ_kin = L/c = 6000/160 ≈ 38 a.** Comparable to τ_V: the wave crosses the glacier in about one melt time, so dynamics can roughly keep pace (validates §1, assumption 5).

Diffusivity needs a slope, not given. Estimate:
- Balance data: b = 0 at the ELA, i.e. 5/0.007 ≈ 714 m above the terminus, so the mean slope must be at least 714/6000 ≈ 0.12; ~0.24 if the ELA is mid-glacier.
- Driving stress: τ_d = ρgH s; with s ≈ 0.15, τ_d ≈ 0.2 MPa (steep but plausible valley glacier), implying surface drop ~900 m ⊇ ELA relief.
- Cross-check: with s ≈ 0.15 and u_s = 40 m a⁻¹, the implied rate factor A ≈ 6×10⁻¹⁷ Pa⁻³ a⁻¹, i.e. normal temperate ice. Consistent.

So take **D = n q/s = 3 × 4800 / 0.15 ≈ 1×10⁵ m² a⁻¹** (range (0.5–2)×10⁵ for s = 0.1–0.3):

**τ_diff = L²/D = (6×10³)²/10⁵ ≈ 3.7×10² ≈ 4×10² a** (range ~250–600 a).

Ranking: τ_V ≈ 30 a ≈ τ_kin ≈ 38 a ≪ τ_D ≈ 4×10² a. Since D < cL (~10×), diffusion — not advection — sets the pace of profile/length adjustment.

## 3. Elevation feedback and the effective response time

Linear model for the mean thickness anomaly δH (fixed bed and shape, so ∂S/∂t = ∂H/∂t; balance linear with β and fixed ELA). Two competing terms in the balance anomaly:

- dynamic damping from §1: −δH/τ_V,
- surface feedback, from ∂b/∂t = β ∂S/∂t: +β δH,
- climate forcing: a (< 0 for warming, m a⁻¹).

dδH/dt = a − (1/τ_V − β) δH → **τ_eff = 1/(1/τ_V − β) = τ_V/(1 − β τ_V)**, and the steady response to forcing a is amplified by (1 − βτ_V)⁻¹.

Numbers: βτ_V = 0.007 × 30 = 0.21 → **τ_eff = 30/0.79 ≈ 38 a** (+27%), amplification ≈ 1.27. The feedback alone would grow on 1/β ≈ 143 a; the damping (30 a) acts faster, so the feedback only stretches, never dominates.

Runaway condition: β > 1/τ_V ⟺ βH > −b_t ⟺ the balance gained by lifting the whole surface by one ice thickness (βH = 1.05 m a⁻¹) exceeds the terminus ablation (5 m a⁻¹). Here 0.21 < 1: stable with a ~5× margin. At β ≥ 1/τ_V the eigenvalue β − 1/τ_V flips sign: both growth and loss anomalies self-amplify (runaway growth or runaway loss).

Why bed slope and geometry changes matter:
1. The damping 1/τ_V is itself dynamic: SIA discharge grows ∝ sⁿ H^(n+2). A steep bed drains excess thickness fast (large c and D → short τ_V → stable). On a flat bed the stabilizing discharge vanishes with the slope, and the β feedback can dominate — the classic flat-bed runaway.
2. The feedback assumed δS = δH at fixed geometry. On a sloping bed this is wrong: retreat moves the terminus up the bed (the ice surface drops less than δH, weakening loss feedback), advance moves it down (limiting growth feedback).
3. Area and hypsometry change as extent changes, reweighting where β applies. The real criterion is β_eff(geometry) < 1/τ_dyn(geometry), and both sides evolve, so stability can flip mid-evolution.

## 4. Bias when inverting a flow parameter from 10 years of dH/dt

Mechanism. At the start of the window the glacier is not in steady state: it still carries excess thickness δH₀ relative to its current climate and keeps thinning at ≈ δH₀/τ_eff no matter what the window climate does. With τ_eff ≈ 38 a and a 10-year window, this memory term decays only by e^(−10/38) ≈ 0.77: three quarters of it survives the whole window. Example: δH₀ = 10 m → ~0.25–0.3 m a⁻¹ of memory thinning, comparable to typical observed mean dH/dt (order −0.5 m a⁻¹). A steady-state-initialized model has no memory term, so this negative rate is unexplained and gets absorbed by the flow parameter — pushed in the direction that thins faster under the given forcing (faster dynamic drawdown, and via the §3 feedback, lower surface → more melt).

Sign for a genuinely retreating glacier: extra negative memory → the inferred parameter is biased toward **too soft / too fast** — creep rate factor A too high, or sliding coefficient too high, i.e. effective viscosity too low (a compensating error: the model then projects future retreat too fast; an advancing-from-memory glacier would bias the other way).

Two practical ways to reduce the bias:
1. Transient spin-up with reconstructed forcing: replace the steady assumption with a hindcast driven by observed/reconstructed climate (temperature, records) started from a documented near-steady epoch (e.g., the 1970s–80s or the LIA maximum), so the model state at t₀ already carries the committed imbalance. The window no longer needs the parameter to explain the drift.
2. Make the initial state a joint unknown and/or pin the parameter independently: invert a correction to the initial geometry or flux-divergence field together with the flow parameter; add observations that constrain the parameter directly (surface velocities at t₀ are best: they constrain A/sliding through deformation, not through the thinning residual); use earlier DEMs/geodetic differences to extend the window backwards into the memory era.

With 10 a of data and τ_eff ≈ 38 a, no parameter tuning can reconstruct the missing state — it can only trade errors. The fix must come from initialization or data.

Summary for this glacier:
- The dominant scale is the volume/kinematic pair: τ_V ≈ 30 a (τ_eff ≈ 38 a with the elevation feedback) and τ_kin ≈ 38 a — they set how fast volume and thickness follow the climate, and they agree.
- The diffusive timescale τ_D ≈ 4×10² a is far longer and only sets the slow tail: full shape and length adjustment, i.e., committed loss beyond the observable decades.
- In practice the ~30–40 a scale matters most: a 10-year dH/dt record spans only a quarter of it, so most of what is observed is delayed history, not the current climate.
