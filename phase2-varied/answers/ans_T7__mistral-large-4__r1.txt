1. Target

I would pick `SIA2D_D_hybrid`. The Lux network takes as inputs the local state and climate at each grid cell and time: thickness `H`, surface slope `|∇s|` (or the staggered-grid gradient used by `SIA2D!`), surface elevation `s`, and the mass balance `MB` from the frozen MB model, plus static features like bed elevation or a glacier id embedding if I want per-glacier behavior. It outputs a dimensionless correction factor `f ≥ 0`, and the diffusivity becomes `D = f · D_physical(H, |∇s|)`, where `D_physical` is the standard SIA diffusivity from Glen's law. To keep the output physical I apply a softplus (or exp) on the raw network output so `f` is strictly positive, and I can clamp or penalize extreme values in the loss.

What each target can and cannot identify:
- `SIA2D_A_target`: the network outputs Glen's `A` directly. It identifies the full rheology, but with no physics left in it — any error in mass balance or in the bed shows up directly as wrong `A`, and `A` has units that are hard to constrain.
- `SIA2D_D_pure`: the network outputs the diffusivity `D` directly. It gives up the scaling `D ∝ H^{n+2} |∇s|^{n-1}`, so the network must relearn physics that we already know, and it can produce non-physical `D` (wrong sign, wrong scaling with `H`).
- `SIA2D_D_hybrid`: keeps the known SIA scaling and learns only the residual factor. It cannot represent a wrong exponent `n` or a fundamentally different flow law — that physics is given up — but in exchange it is far more stable and identifiable from sparse data.

2. Gradient

For a UDE trained over 15 years of `SIA2D!` steps with mutating caches, I would use `DiscreteVJP` (Mooncake) as the default: it does reverse-mode AD over the actual discrete solver, so it is exact for the code as written, handles mutation of `SIA2DCache`, and does not require me to derive anything. Cost is roughly one forward + one reverse pass per training step, with memory proportional to the saved state trajectory. `EnzymeVJP` is also exact and can be faster, but it is more fragile with Julia mutation and heavy solver loops; compile times are painful. `ContinuousVJP` (analytic adjoint of the PDE) is the cheapest in memory and fast, but I must hand-derive the adjoint of `SIA2D!`, and any change to the law or the solver silently invalidates it — high risk here. The `sensealg` and `optim_autoAD` fields in `UDEparameters` only pick the AD backend for the inner UDE solve and the outer optimizer; I would keep them consistent with the chosen VJP and not mix backends.

Validation before a long run: finite-difference check of the loss gradient with respect to a few network parameters (or a subset of them) on a short 1–2 year simulation, comparing VJP gradient vs central differences; then a gradient-norm sanity check on the full setup for one step. If they disagree beyond tolerance, stop and fix before training.

3. Loss

I would use `MultiLoss` combining a `LossH` and a `LossV` with separate weights. `LossH` runs at all 15 yearly timesteps over the gridded thickness; `LossV` runs only at the 3 velocity years. Units: normalize each term — e.g. divide thickness residuals by the mean observed thickness (or its std) and velocity residuals by mean speed — and tune the weights so neither dominates; otherwise the larger-magnitude term wins regardless of information content. Data gaps: mask cells where observations are missing (NaN) and give them zero weight, so the loss and its gradient ignore them. Velocity at only some times still constrains the whole history: the adjoint backpropagates from the velocity mismatch through all intermediate `SIA2D!` steps to the initial state and the network, because later states depend on earlier ones. Near ice margins things go wrong easily: slope goes to zero, `D ∝ |∇s|^{n-1}` becomes singular/flat, thickness is near zero, and small model errors produce huge relative residuals. I would mask or down-weight margin cells, use relative (not absolute) residuals there, and watch for gradient spikes.

4. Mass balance

I would use the calibrated `TImodel1` from Muninn via `calibrate_MB_model`. It is physically constrained (degree-day factor, precipitation factor), calibrated against point or geodetic data, and its errors are spatially smooth and interpretable. The frozen `CustomMLP` is fast and maybe more accurate at point scale, but it is a black box: its errors are arbitrary in space and time and, because it is frozen, the inversion cannot correct them — so the learned law absorbs them. Detection: compare the learned correction `f` (or `A`) against independent evidence — geodetic mass balance, winter/summer balance gradients, or the calibrated TImodel — look for spatial correlation between MB residuals and learned-law anomalies, test on held-out glaciers or years, and check that the learned law does not drift to compensate a constant MB bias (e.g. a spin-up or fixed-point test where MB bias should not change `f` much).

5. Top three AD pitfalls

1. Mutating caches: `SIA2D!` mutates the cache/results in place; with a VJP that cannot handle mutation (or a hand-written adjoint that assumes purity) you get silently wrong gradients. Use Mooncake/`DiscreteVJP`, or make the mutation explicit and tested.
2. `@ignore_derivatives` misuse: it zeros the gradient through a region. If placed around code that actually influences the loss (or its argument list changes), training stalls or drifts with no error. Use it only for genuinely non-differentiable or gradient-irrelevant code, and verify with the finite-difference check.
3. Non-differentiable or type-unstable operations in the law/loss: indexing with data-dependent indices, `if` on arrays, `max`/clamps in the wrong place, or allocations that change types between forward and reverse. These break Enzyme/Mooncake or give NaNs only in the backward pass; validate gradients early and keep the network output smooth (softplus, no hard branches).

One caveat: I am confident about the overall design (targets, VJP choice, loss structure), but I would double-check the exact keyword names in `UDEparameters` and the loss constructors against the ODINN.jl source before writing code, rather than trust my memory of the API.
